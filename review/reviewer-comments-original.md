# Reviewer comments (author-provided)

## Reviewer 1

This manuscript investigates the molecular basis of oxaliplatin-induced peripheral neuropathy (OIPN) using a systems-biology approach followed by in vivo validation. The authors mined two publicly available rat dorsal root ganglion (DRG) RNA-sequencing datasets, a nerve-compression injury model and an oxaliplatin model, and applied WGCNA and differential expression analysis, intersecting the results to identify 63 genes shared between the two conditions. Functional enrichment linked these genes to cell-cycle regulation and cellular senescence, and PPI network analysis nominated Cdk1, Cdc20, and Cdkn1a as central hub genes. The authors then established their own rat OIPN model, confirmed mechanical allodynia and thermal hypersensitivity, and used qPCR on bulk DRG tissue to show upregulation of these three genes alongside the inflammatory mediators IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α. From this they conclude that oxaliplatin triggers aberrant reactivation of cell-cycle machinery in post-mitotic sensory neurons, driving a senescence-like inflammatory state that contributes to neuropathic pain.

The paper should be rejected because its central conclusion is not supported by any of the data presented. Every measurement in the study, both the mined datasets and the authors' own qPCR, derives from bulk DRG homogenate, a tissue in which neurons are a minority population surrounded by satellite glia, Schwann cells, and infiltrating immune cells. The signature the authors report, upregulation of mitotic genes (Cdk1, Cdc20) and inflammatory cytokines, is precisely what a proliferating glial and immune compartment produces after nerve injury, and is far more parsimoniously explained by these dividing non-neuronal cells than by cell-cycle re-entry in post-mitotic neurons. The authors provide no cell-type-resolved evidence (no co-immunostaining, in situ hybridization, or single-cell data) to localize a single transcript to a neuron, yet the neuronal interpretation is the entire thesis of the paper, stated in its title. Compounding this, the study rests on a logical inconsistency: intersecting an oxaliplatin model with a mechanical-injury model selects for the generic, shared injury response common to any nerve insult, which actively removes oxaliplatin-specific biology rather than illuminating it, so the shared-gene strategy manufactures the very cell-cycle and inflammation signal the authors then over-interpret. Finally, the work measures only mRNA abundance while drawing conclusions about the enzymatic activity of cell-cycle proteins, machinery that is regulated almost entirely at the post-translational level (phosphorylation, complex assembly, checkpoint control); transcript levels of Cdk1 or Cdc20 say nothing about whether the cell cycle is actually active. With no cell-type localization, no protein or activity data, and a discovery strategy biased against its own conclusion, the manuscript's core claim remains an unsupported assumption rather than a demonstrated mechanism.

## Reviewer 2

The authors of this study performed an integrated systems biology analysis combining comparative transcriptomics, WGCNA, differential expression analysis, and protein–protein interaction network analysis to identify molecular signatures shared between oxaliplatin-induced peripheral neuropathy (OIPN) and nerve compression neuropathy. They identified Cdk1, Cdc20, and Cdkn1a as candidate hub genes and further demonstrated their increased mRNA expression, together with inflammatory mediators, in the dorsal root ganglia of an oxaliplatin-treated rat model. The research concept is interesting and potentially provides novel insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying OIPN. The manuscript is also generally well written and the combination of computational analyses and in vivo validation is potentially valuable.

However, I have major concerns regarding the extent to which the central mechanistic conclusions are supported by the experimental data. In particular, although the authors propose that Cdk1, Cdc20, and Cdkn1a are upregulated in post-mitotic sensory neurons and that their dysregulation contributes to aberrant cell-cycle re-entry, senescence-like responses, neuroinflammation, and neuropathic pain, the current data do not directly establish these mechanistic relationships. Moreover, the comparative transcriptomic strategy does not distinguish mechanisms specifically associated with OIPN from molecular responses shared with other forms of neuropathic injury.

These issues concern the central premise and interpretation of the study rather than isolated aspects of presentation or minor methodological details. Substantial additional experimental validation would therefore be required to support the proposed mechanistic model. Alternatively, if such experiments are not feasible, the central conclusions would need to be substantially revised and considerably tempered. In its current form, I do not believe that the manuscript provides sufficient evidence to support its principal mechanistic claims. Below, I outline several major concerns that should be addressed.

### Major Comment

**Comment 1 —Distinction between OIPN-specific mechanisms and mechanisms shared with neuropathic injury**

The comparative transcriptomic strategy identifies genes that are commonly altered in both OIPN and nerve compression neuropathy. Although this approach may be useful for identifying molecular mechanisms shared across different forms of neuropathic injury, it does not necessarily identify mechanisms that are specific to OIPN. Therefore, the rationale for using the common genes between these two models as key molecular candidates for OIPN requires further justification.

In particular, the authors state that focusing on commonly altered genes may reduce “model-specific bias and false-positive findings.” This interpretation appears overly simplistic. Molecular alterations that are specific to the OIPN model should not necessarily be regarded as false-positive findings, as such alterations may represent biologically meaningful mechanisms specific to oxaliplatin-induced neurotoxicity. Conversely, genes shared between OIPN and nerve compression neuropathy may reflect general responses to neuronal injury or neuropathic pain rather than OIPN-specific mechanisms.

The authors should therefore substantially revise the interpretation of the comparative analysis throughout the manuscript. Specifically, the authors should clearly distinguish between molecular mechanisms shared across neuropathic conditions and mechanisms specifically associated with OIPN. The authors should also reconsider, and appropriately modify, statements describing Cdk1, Cdc20, and Cdkn1a as specific molecular drivers or key pathogenic mechanisms of OIPN, unless additional evidence is provided to demonstrate their OIPN-specific roles. The limitations of the current comparative strategy should also be explicitly addressed in the Discussion.

**Comment 2 — Cellular localization**

The authors propose that aberrant cell-cycle signaling occurs in post-mitotic sensory neurons. However, the validation experiments were performed using bulk DRG tissue, which contains multiple cell types. The current data therefore cannot establish that the observed changes in Cdk1, Cdc20, and Cdkn1a originate from sensory neurons. Cellular localization experiments would substantially strengthen the mechanistic interpretation.

**Comment 3 — Cell-cycle re-entry**

Increased expression of Cdk1, Cdc20, and Cdkn1a does not by itself demonstrate functional cell-cycle re-entry. The authors should distinguish between altered expression of cell-cycle-related genes and actual cell-cycle activation/re-entry and moderate the mechanistic claims accordingly. If feasible, additional evidence of cell-cycle activation would strengthen the study.

**Comment 4 — Senescence**

The conclusion regarding a senescence-like phenotype is currently based primarily on Cdkn1a upregulation and pathway enrichment. Because Cdkn1a is also induced by DNA damage and other cellular stress responses, additional senescence-associated markers would be desirable. Alternatively, the authors should present the senescence interpretation explicitly as a hypothesis rather than a demonstrated mechanism.

**Comment 5 — Causality**

The current study demonstrates an association between OIPN, cell-cycle-related gene expression, and inflammatory gene expression, but does not establish causality. Functional modulation of Cdk1, Cdc20, or Cdkn1a would substantially strengthen the claim that these genes contribute to neuropathic pain. If such experiments are beyond the scope of the current study, the causal language and the statement that these genes represent therapeutic targets should be moderated.

**Comment 6 — Temporal relationship**

The proposed sequence of Cdk1/Cdc20 activation, Cdkn1a-mediated cell-cycle arrest, senescence-like changes, and inflammatory activation is not directly established. Because the molecular analyses appear to have been performed at a limited time point, the temporal relationship among these events remains unclear. The authors should acknowledge this limitation.

**Comment 7 — Alternative interpretation**

The authors should discuss the possibility that Cdk1, Cdc20, and Cdkn1a upregulation represents a secondary response to neuronal injury rather than a primary driver of OIPN. Similarly, the observed inflammatory changes may occur independently of cell-cycle dysregulation.
